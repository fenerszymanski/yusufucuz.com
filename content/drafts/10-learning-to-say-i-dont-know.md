# Learning to say I don't know

Before my first tour I was afraid of exactly one thing.

Not of being boring. Not of losing my route, or of nobody turning up at the clock. I was afraid that somebody would ask me a question I could not answer, in front of everyone, and that the whole thing would come apart in the four seconds afterwards.

I have written elsewhere about leaving a marketing career to do this. That fear is the one honest piece of self-knowledge I brought with me: I knew that I was a man explaining a city he did not grow up in, and I knew what the weak point was.

Then it happened.

## The thing nobody tells you

The fear assumes that the group is there to test you. They are not. Almost nobody on a walking tour is hoping to catch the guide out, and the one person in a hundred who is will do it regardless of how much you have read.

What the group actually wants to know is much simpler: can I trust what this person says. And the fastest way to answer that question is to be visibly willing to say when you cannot.

I did not believe this for a long time. It sounds like the sort of comfortable thing you tell yourself after a bad moment. But I have now watched it work often enough to be confident about it: three honest words cost less credibility than one confident sentence that turns out to be approximate.

## The reflex to resist

There is a move available to every guide, and it is very tempting. You take the question you cannot answer and quietly convert it into a nearby question you can. *That's a great question, and what's interesting is that in 1871...*

Everyone can hear you doing it. The person who asked knows they have been handled. And the group learns something about you that you did not intend to teach them, which is that there are places you will not go.

The other version costs four seconds of discomfort. Say you do not know. Say what you do know that is adjacent to it, clearly marked as adjacent. Offer to find out. Then, if you said you would find out, actually do it, because a promise to look something up is worth nothing unless the person gets the answer.

## Why this matters more here than elsewhere

Berlin is a city where being approximately right is not good enough.

Almost every serious subject on my route touches something that people are still arguing about, still living with, or still descended from. In that context a confident guess is not a small error. It is a small act of disrespect towards the thing you are standing in front of.

So the rule I work by is the same one I use when preparing: if I cannot say where I learned it, I do not say it on tour. The corollary is the sentence I was so frightened of, and it turns out to be the most useful one I own.

I still do not enjoy saying it. I just no longer think it is the failure I thought it was.
