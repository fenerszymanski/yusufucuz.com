# What a free tour actually costs

"Free walking tour" is one of the most effective phrases in travel, and I use it, because it is true in the only sense that matters to you: you can join, and you owe nothing.

It is also the phrase that quietly sets the price before anyone arrives. That is the part worth explaining, and I have never managed to explain it properly while walking backwards through a crowd.

## How it actually works

You book. You show up. We walk for around two hours. At the end you decide what it was worth, and you pay that, or you pay nothing and leave, and I do not follow you down the street.

That last part is real. There is no minimum, no card with a suggested amount, no hand extended in a doorway. If the tour was not good you should not pay for it.

And here is the thing people get wrong about this model: almost everyone does pay. Participation is not the problem. In my experience it is close to universal, and I have stopped expecting otherwise.

The problem is the size of it.

## Where the gap comes from

A two-hour tour is not two hours of work. It sits on ten or more hours a week of reading, checking, re-checking what has physically changed on the route, and hunting for the photograph or the map that will make an eighteenth-century square legible to someone standing in it in 2026. That is before the website, the photographs, the emails, and the booking admin, all of which are also me.

None of that is visible to you. You meet a man with an umbrella who appears to know things. The years underneath that are invisible by design, because a tour where the guide keeps telling you how hard he worked is an unbearable tour.

So the gap is not between generous people and stingy ones. It is between two hours of apparent effort and two years of actual effort, and the second number is not in the room.

I think there are three reasons the sums come out where they do, and none of them is meanness.

**The word "free."** It arrives before I do. By the time we meet, a number has already formed in the mind, and everything after that is an adjustment to it. That is not the visitor's fault. It is the model's.

**The invisibility of the work.** See above. The better I am at hiding it, the less it appears to be worth.

**Nobody knows the norm.** This is the one I have the most sympathy for. Half the people on any given tour come from countries with no tipping culture at all. They are not deciding to underpay. They genuinely do not know what the number is supposed to be, and there is no polite way to ask.

The result is that a good tour, at the end of a good morning, can be answered with less than what the coffee across the square costs. Not always. Not even usually. But often enough that it is the defining fact of the job rather than an occasional bad day.

## The three things that actually cost

If you asked me what is expensive about this work, I would not start with money.

**Not knowing.** You do not find out what your morning was worth until it is over. The same route, the same preparation, the same weather, delivered to two different groups, produce two very different mornings. You cannot plan against it and you cannot get better at predicting it.

**Doing all of it.** There is no colleague. Every part of this, from the route to the invoice, is one person, and the tour is the only part anyone sees.

**Having to be good every time.** This is the real one. Nobody has paid in advance, so every group decides from scratch whether the last two hours were worth anything. There is no credit from last week. There is no room for an off day. That is an uncomfortable arrangement, and it is also, I think, the reason I am better at this than I would be on a fixed ticket price.

## Why I keep the model anyway

Because it keeps the pressure in the right place. If you have already paid, I have your money whether the next ninety minutes are good or not. If you have not, the only thing between me and a wasted morning is whether I am worth listening to.

And because it keeps the tour open. Students, families counting every euro, people at the end of a long trip who have run out of money and not out of curiosity. They walk with me and they pay what they can, and I would much rather have them there than have a price at the door that quietly removes them.

## If you are the one deciding

Pay what it was worth to you. Not what you think is expected, because there is no expected. If you learned something, if you would recommend it to a friend, that number is probably a little higher than the one in your hand.

And if it was mediocre, pay accordingly, and tell me why. I would genuinely rather have that than a polite few coins and silence.
