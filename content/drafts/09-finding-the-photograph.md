# Finding the photograph that makes 1780 visible

The hardest thing about guiding in Berlin's historic centre is that most of what I am describing is not there.

The medieval town is gone. The palace was gone, then rebuilt as something else. Whole streets were bombed, cleared, replaced, and in some cases cleared again by a second government with the opposite politics. I spend two hours pointing at things that were demolished, in front of buildings that replaced them, for people who are standing in 2026 and being asked to imagine 1780.

You cannot do that with dates. I have tried. Dates slide off.

So the actual craft of this job, the part I spend the most time on, is finding the one image that does the work a paragraph cannot.

## What that looks like on the tour

I carry old photographs and maps and hold them up in front of the thing they show.

That is the whole technique. It is not clever. But the moment someone lines up a photograph with the view in front of them and finds the one surviving corner, something happens that no amount of narration produces. They stop being told about a place and start seeing two versions of it at once.

I only learned how central this was from my reviews. Guests describe it more precisely than I would: one wrote that it felt more like a story session than a tour, another that it made the city possible to picture rather than just a list of facts. Nobody wrote about my dates being correct. They assume that, and they are right to. Accuracy is the floor, not the product.

## Where the images come from

Three places, in this order.

**Archives and museum collections.** This is the good material and it is slower to find. Public collections have digitised an enormous amount, and the metadata is usually reliable enough to know what you are actually looking at, which matters more than it sounds.

**Books.** A surprising number of the best images are still only in print. If I find one in a book I go looking for its source, because a scan with no provenance is a picture, not evidence.

**Ordinary searching.** Not glamorous, and it is where most of the dead ends live. An image that circulates widely online is often mislabelled, sometimes by a century, sometimes by a city. Anything I find this way has to be confirmed somewhere else before it goes in the bag.

## The rule underneath all of it

**If I cannot say where I learned it, I do not say it on tour.**

This is harder than it sounds, because guiding runs on anecdote and anecdotes travel between guides faster than corrections do. Berlin has an unusual number of these in circulation, partly because so many true things that happened here sound invented that people stop checking.

The cost of being wrong is not that someone corrects me in front of the group. It is that everything else I said that morning becomes suspect, retroactively, and rightly.

## The books I have actually read

I am not going to give you a canon, because I have not read one and pretending otherwise would be exactly the kind of thing this article is against. Two have done real work for me:

**Alexandra Richie, *Faust's Metropolis*.** The single-volume history of the city. Enormous and dense. It is the one that gives you the shape into which everything else fits.

**Anna Funder, *Stasiland*.** Interviews with people on both sides of the East German surveillance state. It is the book that turns the GDR from a system into a set of people who made choices and then had to live in the same country afterwards.

That is a short list and it will grow. I would rather publish two titles I have finished than six I have heard of.

## The German problem

Much of the best material on Berlin is in German, and I work through translation.

I would rather say that plainly than let a reading list imply otherwise. It means I am slower, it means I lean harder on the sources that exist in English, and it is one of the real limits of being someone who came to this city as an adult rather than growing up in it. A guest once wrote as much in a review. He had a point.

What I can do is be careful, be explicit about what I do not know, and keep the standard for what earns a place in the two hours high enough that nobody has to take my word for anything.

## The week before

The routine, every time, is short:

1. **Check what has physically changed.** Berlin renovates constantly. Turning up to a scaffolded building with a group is the most avoidable failure in this job.
2. **Re-check one thing I have been saying for months.** One, chosen at random.
3. **Read something outside the route.** The questions never come from inside it.

None of that is visible on the tour, and it should not be. But it is the difference between a guide who is charming and a guide who is worth two hours of your one weekend here.
